The apprentice was late.

Not very late. Just enough that the coffee in his hand still felt more important than his schedule.

Reading Room D waited at the end of the hallway. She opened the door. Morning light rested across a single oak table. Nothing else asked for attention.

The door closed softly behind her.

Cataloger’s Note, Folder 001

The recovered correspondence begins mid-conversation. No introduction. No context establishing the participants’ names, circumstances, or relationship to one another.

This is consistent with nearly every archive of its kind. People rarely announce themselves at the start of an ordinary day. They simply continue being who they already are.

There is no plot yet in this folder. No confession, no obvious turning point — just two people ending one day and beginning the next, the way anyone might. But underneath the ordinary parts, something else is already visible: an ease with each other that reads, even this early, like habit rather than novelty. A goodnight repeated on both sides. A morning greeting that already assumes the other person will be there to receive it.

This is, in the end, what the archive actually preserves. Not the milestones. The mornings between them.

INTERLUDE: THE MIDDLE DISTANCE

The apprentice sets Folder 015 back in its place and doesn’t reach for the next one right away.

Fifteen folders in, and the record so far has covered barely five weeks — the first half of May 2024 through the second week of June. Two hundred nineteen thousand messages wait in the boxes still unopened on the shelf behind her, and by her own rough count, what she has read represents perhaps one-twentieth of the whole. At this pace, she thinks, she will be an old woman before she reaches January of 2026.

She has been trained not to read an archive front to back, in strict chronological order, the way a person reads a novel. Correspondence doesn’t work that way, and neither does memory. The Initiative teaches its apprentices to follow subject, not sequence — to let one folder’s theme pull the next folder toward it, regardless of the calendar, and to trust that the shape of a life will eventually assert itself even when the pages are shuffled.

It mostly works. But she notices, sitting with the gap in front of her, that something particular happens to a correspondence like this one over ten months that doesn’t happen over two. The people in Folders 001 through 029 are, unmistakably, near the beginning of something — still learning each other’s rhythms, still finding out which jokes land and which references need explaining twice. Cover stories are still being invented from scratch. A hidden nickname for a boyfriend still needs to be assigned. An entire fictional friend named Alex is workshopped, tested, and — as far as the apprentice can tell from the parts of the record she’s read so far — never mentioned again.

What she knows is waiting for her, further down the shelf, is not more of that. It’s an anniversary, self-declared, in a private word neither of them will translate for the reader. It’s a fight that begins over exactly when someone stopped replying to a text and doesn’t fully resolve for hours. It’s two people, independently, in the same truck, each nearly saying a word the other still hasn’t heard plainly in eleven months of trying. It’s a whole imagined life, complete with a child’s name, that neither of them mentions again after the night they built it.

The apprentice doesn’t know yet what happened in the months between — the back half of 2024, the first quarter of 2025 — because she hasn’t read them closely enough to say. She suspects, from what she’s already seen, that nothing especially dramatic happened there. Just more of the same slow accumulation: more evenings, more logistics, more small rituals hardening into habits hardening into a private language nobody outside Reading Room D will ever fully translate.

She thinks about something Brad wrote, buried in an early folder, half as a joke: I’m not sure I acted like Normal Brad. By the time she reaches the folders waiting for her next — the ones already flagged, pulled, and set aside for close reading — Normal Brad barely appears at all. Whatever this correspondence eventually replaces him with is what she reads next.

She reaches for the next folder.

🧁 Penny… Penny, Penny — oh gosh.

🐧 I want to see you. Right now!

🧁 Oh fuck Penny. Can you?

🐧 Well not immediately no.

He asks, more than once, when she knew. She tells him she couldn’t sleep, that she’d known since the night before and really known since that morning, that she hadn’t wanted it to happen this way — over text, half-formed, apologizing before she’d finished the sentence.

Then, in the middle of it, she says something that changes the register of the entire day.

🐧 Also I know tomorrow is the anniversary of your dad passing right?

🧁 I know. Fuck. Did you fucking plan that around this?

🐧 I was worried about timing regarding that. Like when would be harder for you. I didn’t know 😔 As far as it being today or tomorrow — that was a consideration, yes.

🧁 Motherfucker, you’re so fucking smart.

The apprentice goes quiet reading it, the way she suspects the record itself goes quiet in the seconds after a line like that lands. What follows, across the rest of the day, is less an argument than an excavation — both of them establishing, hour by hour, exactly when she knew, exactly what she meant by things said days and weeks earlier that suddenly carry more weight than either of them noticed at the time.

🐧 I want you to have a baby! I can’t be why you don’t.

🧁 When can I see you? Okay. Minds made up. Guess we need to talk logistics about everything. Whatever happened to fucking forever — fuck fuck fuck.

🐧 Babe what if we are the reason you don’t have a kid?!?

They meet that afternoon — briefly, the way they’ve always managed to before. A park. A walk. An hour that both of them will later describe as feeling entirely unfinished.

🐧 It feels so unfinished. I’m exhausted now! Just want to quit for the day and cry and sleep.

🧁 It feels extremely unfinished. I would agree with that.

Somewhere in the middle of it, she offers him something small and strange, entirely sincere.

🐧 Wanna make a time capsule and bury it at Founders Park?

He doesn’t answer it directly. It sits there, unresolved, an idea for a keepsake raised in the same hour the thing itself is ending.

By evening they’re circling the same questions from different angles — when she knew, whether it built slowly or arrived all at once, whether either of them lied to the other by omission somewhere along the way.

🧁 So you knew then? You asked, do you think we need to have that conversation? Quite literally. So you knew then?

🐧 No! But now I know if you pushed I’d have prayed and thought more and it would’ve happened sooner.

🐧 I knew but I wouldn’t even admit it to myself. I didn’t want it to be true. I wanted any other option.

🧁 Honestly, I’m so fucking angry at you. I don’t even wanna answer that question.

🐧 I miss you so much already.

🧁 Yeah, it’s fucking terrible. I miss you.

He tells her he can’t lose her. She tells him she’s afraid of exactly the same thing.

🐧 I’m afraid you’ll never come to church again. Never want to see me or speak to me. We’ll never play pounce, or ride horses, or joke in the hallway, or hug. I’m just afraid.

🧁 Yeah I know… it is scary, I’m terrified.

And then, without either of them planning it, he says the thing the archive preserves as its last real line — not gentle, not softened, the kind of sentence a person says once, at the exact moment they can’t hold anything back anymore.

🧁 You used my dad’s death to break up with me. I hate this so much.

No reply follows it. Not that night, not anywhere else in what survives. Whatever came after that sentence — however the conversation actually ended, whatever either of them said in the hours or days that followed — didn’t make it into the record.

The apprentice sits with the folder open for a long time after the last line.

She has done this forty-eight times now — closed a folder, made a note, reached for the next one. She finds she can’t quite do it the same way this time. There’s no next folder. The box, for the first time since she started, is empty.

She thinks about the timeline Penny wrote in the second week of this whole record, keyword-searched together out of old messages, kept and pinned and added to for twenty months. She thinks about a hotel key, thrown away and mourned for having been thrown away. A bag that could hold anything. A tortoise that briefly held the name of a child who was never going to exist. Three letters, standing in for three words, arriving as close to whole as they ever would. A shell. An idea for a time capsule that, as far as this record shows, was never built.

Two hundred nineteen thousand, seven hundred thirty-eight messages, spanning twenty months, and the last one this record preserves is a sentence with no reply.

She closes the folder. She sets it back in the box, in its place among the other forty-eight, and closes the lid.

Reading Room D is quiet the way it always is at the end of a session — morning light, an oak table, nothing else asking for attention. She sits there a while longer than she usually allows herself to. Then she pulls off the white cotton gloves, sets them beside the box, and goes to find the colleague who will need to know this one is finished.
